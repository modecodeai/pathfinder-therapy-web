# How does EMDR work?

**Category:** Therapy Explained · **Read time:** 7 min

*EMDR is a structured trauma therapy that supports the brain and body to process memories that still feel active.*

---

EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing. The name is technical, but the problem it addresses is deeply human: some experiences do not settle. Years later they still arrive with full colour, full charge and full urgency — as if the past has not yet learned that it is over.

EMDR is often used when experiences remain vivid, intrusive or emotionally charged. The aim is not to erase memory. The aim is to help the nervous system relate to the memory differently.

## Why some memories stay "live".

To understand EMDR, it helps to understand what it is trying to work with.

Ordinarily, experiences are processed and filed. Something difficult happens, we feel it, we make sense of it, we talk about it, we sleep on it, and over time it becomes a memory with a date attached. It belongs to the past.

One model used in EMDR describes distressing experiences as memories that have not become fully integrated with what we know now. This is a clinical way of understanding the problem, rather than a literal account of how every memory is stored. Images, sensations, emotions or beliefs can feel intensely present. A smell, a tone of voice or a passing thought may trigger a response that feels connected to an earlier experience.

Telling someone to "just move on" does not address that distress. Therapy aims to help the person respond to the memory with a stronger sense of the present.

## What EMDR actually involves.

In an EMDR session, once preparation is complete, you are asked to bring a specific memory to mind — the image, the emotion, the body sensation and the belief that goes with it (something like "I'm not safe" or "it was my fault"). While you hold that in awareness, the therapist guides you through sets of bilateral stimulation: typically side-to-side eye movements, alternating taps or tones.

Between sets, you notice what comes up and share what feels relevant with your therapist. You do not need to describe every detail or make yourself feel more than you feel. The aim is to reduce the distress connected with the memory and support a more helpful understanding of the experience in the present.

Over the course of the work, the memory typically loses its charge. People often describe the same event feeling "further away", "smaller" or "like it belongs to the past now". The facts remain. The felt urgency fades.

## Why the eye movements?

The honest answer is that researchers are still refining the explanation, but several strands of evidence converge.

There are different explanations for how eye movements and the wider therapy may help. Proposed mechanisms should not be mistaken for a complete or settled account. EMDR is a structured psychological therapy that includes assessment, preparation, memory processing and review; it is more than an eye-movement exercise.

NICE recommends EMDR for adults with PTSD or clinically important PTSD symptoms in specified circumstances. That evidence does not mean EMDR is the right treatment for every person or every difficulty. The guidance and patient information linked below explain its use for PTSD.

## It should feel structured and collaborative.

Good EMDR preparation matters. The work includes history-taking, resourcing, consent, pacing and regular checking-in.

EMDR is described as an eight-phase approach, and most of those phases are not eye movements at all. Before any processing begins, we take time to understand your history, identify what we are working towards, and build resources — ways of grounding and steadying yourself that you can use in and out of sessions. Processing only begins when there is enough stability to support it.

You can ask to pause or stop at any point, using a signal agreed with your therapist. Your feedback shapes the pace. If something feels too fast or you feel disconnected, we pause and return to grounding or preparation as needed.

## What it feels like from the inside.

Experiences differ. EMDR does not use hypnosis, and there is no requirement to perform distress. The therapist helps you stay connected with the present while noticing the memory, and checks how you are managing throughout.

During the sets, people may notice images shifting, memories surfacing, thoughts changing or emotions rising and passing. Some sessions feel intense; others feel quiet. These experiences do not tell us exactly what is happening in the brain. My role is to attend to your responses, check the pace and help bring the session to a manageable close.

People can notice changes in emotion, sleep or the memories that come to mind between sessions. We discuss what you might experience and agree how to manage distress and seek support. If reactions feel overwhelming, persist or affect your safety, contact your therapist or an appropriate health service; they should not simply be dismissed as a normal part of treatment.

## How long does it take?

EMDR can sometimes be a focused, short-term piece of therapy. For other people it forms part of longer work. The total includes assessment, preparation and review as well as memory processing. We agree the focus and review progress together rather than promise a fixed number of sessions or a particular result.

Preparation time also varies from person to person. Someone with steady resources may move to processing quickly; someone whose nervous system has been braced for decades may need longer to build the stability that makes processing safe rather than re-traumatising. That slower start is not lost time. It is the foundation everything else stands on.

## Is EMDR only for trauma?

EMDR is best established as a treatment for PTSD. Distressing experiences and the difficulties connected with them can take many forms, so an assessment matters more than whether a person uses the word "trauma" about their own experience.

EMDR is also explored for some other memory-related difficulties, but the evidence and treatment recommendations differ by problem. We would discuss the reasons for considering it, alternatives and any limitations. Where a person's difficulties are complex, EMDR may be integrated into longer relational work.

## Where EMDR sits in my work.

EMDR is one possible tool within therapy, not a performance and not a shortcut around relationship.

In my practice, EMDR sits inside a relational, trauma-informed frame. The method is never more important than the relationship. Some people come specifically for EMDR and we contract for focused work. For others, it emerges as one strand within longer therapy — used when a particular memory keeps interrupting the wider work, then set aside again.

EMDR is not hypnosis or a way to erase memories. The aim is to change how distressing experiences affect you now. People respond differently, and we review whether the approach is helping.

## Common worries, answered briefly.

Will I lose control? EMDR is not hypnosis. You and your therapist agree how to pause or stop. Tell your therapist if you feel overwhelmed or disconnected so that the work can return to grounding, preparation or a different approach.

What if I cannot handle what comes up? Preparation includes practising ways to steady yourself and agreeing a plan for distress. Your therapist reviews readiness with you; beginning processing is not an obligation to continue if it becomes too much.

What if I do not remember things clearly? You do not need to force a complete story. The therapist works with what you can report and respects uncertainty. Images, sensations or thoughts that arise during therapy are not, by themselves, proof that a particular event happened.

## How to know if it might help.

If there are specific experiences that still feel active — memories you avoid, images that intrude, situations your body reacts to out of all proportion to the present — EMDR may be worth exploring. If what you carry is more diffuse, the answer may still be yes, but the route in will be slower and more relational.
